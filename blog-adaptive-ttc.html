NOTE 11 · TEST-TIME COMPUTE & SYSTEMS 8 min read

Turning Idle GPU Bandwidth Into 3× Reasoning Accuracy (and Two Ideas That Failed)

In Note 07, we squeezed more tokens per second out of our RTX 5090. Next we asked a harder question: given a fixed GPU and open weights, how do you multiply the intelligence produced per second? Here is what jumped Qwen3.8-27B from 31.2% to 93.8% on competition-grade math and code, and two hyped research ideas we tested, measured, and threw away.

ABSTRACT

We mined 1,715 recent AI research papers in the Space AI database for practical test-time scaling techniques and benchmarked them end-to-end on a single RTX 5090 running Qwen3.8-27B and Inkling-2-Mini. On a 16-problem suite of programmatically verified AIME/Olympiad math, combinatorics, and code-tracing tasks, we discovered that our live default (reasoning_effort="none") scored just 31.2% (5/16). Adding intent-aware thinking jumped single-pass accuracy to 81.2% (13/16) while keeping simple factual QA at ~180 ms. Exploiting Blackwell's memory-bandwidth roofline to run 4 parallel reasoning trajectories inside Basalt's 8-slot continuous batcher, combined with DeepConf tail-confidence weighting, 3-of-4 early quorum straggler cancellation, and a conditional Generative Verifier (GenRM) invoked on only 19% of queries, lifted accuracy to 93.8% (15/16) at 597.5 tok/s aggregate for just +8.8% wall-clock latency (10.86 s → 11.82 s). Conversely, two plausible engine-level ideas (varying Inkling-2-Mini's recurrent loop depth (loops=2,4) and flattening Basalt's speculative-decoding cost prior for GPU-resident MoE) failed under measurement and were reverted.

1. Why a fast GPU still leaves most of its brain idle

If you took a Computer Architecture course, you have seen the roofline model: every program is limited either by how fast the processor can do math (compute-bound) or how fast it can haul bytes from memory (memory-bandwidth-bound).

Generating text one token at a time from a single prompt (batch size N = 1) is brutally memory-bandwidth-bound. To generate one token with Qwen3.8-27B, our RTX 5090 has to stream roughly 16.5 GB of quantized weights from GDDR7 VRAM through the tensor cores, multiply them by a single activation vector, and throw the weights away until the next token. The tensor cores sit idle waiting on memory reads.

Think of it like driving a 64-seat bus with 1 passenger. Running 4 independent reasoning attempts at the same time (N = 4) via continuous batching reads the exact same 16.5 GB of weights from VRAM once and multiplies them against 4 activation vectors in parallel. On our live RTX 5090 server, scaling from N = 1 to N = 8 concurrent requests jumps aggregate throughput from ~204-419 tok/s up to 719-965 tok/s.

That raises an obvious systems question: can we spend that almost-free parallel batch capacity to make a single user's answer dramatically smarter?

2. First, we caught a massive silent bug in our own default

Before testing fancy algorithms, we built a clean 16-problem benchmark where every ground-truth answer is computed directly in Python, covering nested modular towers (7^(7^7) mod 1000), constrained derangements, Catalan grid paths with forbidden points, Markov chain hitting times, Matrix-Tree spanning tree counts, subtraction Nim, and tricky loop state tracing. No LLM-as-a-judge fluff: either the integer matches or it doesn't.

When we ran our live production server as-is, Qwen3.8-27B scored a dismal 5 / 16 (31.2%). Why? Because our config defaulted to reasoning_effort="none" so simple chat prompts felt snappy. Without its hidden chain-of-thought scratchpad, the model tried to solve Olympiad combinatorics directly in prose, rambling for 1,500+ tokens and botching arithmetic halfway through.

Simply flipping reasoning_effort to "medium" jumped single-pass accuracy by +50 percentage points to 13 / 16 (81.2%). Even better, on a separate 8-problem diagnostic sweep, "medium" actually used 34% fewer total tokens than "none" (990 vs. 1,501) because concise private scratchpad math is shorter than verbose prose guesswork!

The fix (Towards Thinking-Optimal Scaling): We added a zero-latency intent classifier (classify_reasoning_effort) in our router. Trivial lookups like "What is the capital of France?" stay at "none" and finish in ~180 ms; math, code, proofs, and multi-step prompts automatically upgrade to "medium" unless the caller explicitly pins an effort level.

3. Building space-think: three recent papers in 250 lines of async Python

Single-pass thinking still made mistakes on 3 of the 16 hard problems (e.g., missing a prime-power case when counting integers ≤ 500 with exactly 12 divisors, answering 11 instead of 44). To close that gap without blowing up latency, we combined three techniques from our papers database into a single module (network/adaptive_ttc.py):

  1. 4-Slot Parallel Solving (Exploiting the Bandwidth Roofline): We dispatch N = 4 concurrent trajectories to Basalt's 8-slot continuous batcher: solver 0 runs deterministically (temperature = 0.0) as a greedy anchor, while solvers 1-3 sample diverse reasoning paths (temperature = 0.55, distinct seeds and checklist hints) with token logprobs enabled. Because the GPU reads weights once per batch step, all 4 trajectories finish together at 597.5 tok/s aggregate, only +0.96 s slower than running 1 trajectory alone.
  2. DeepConf Bottom-10% Tail Confidence Weighting: Standard majority voting gives a shaky hallucination the exact same vote as a rock-solid proof. Instead, we inspect the per-token log-probabilities returned by the model and score each trajectory by the geometric mean of its lowest 10% token probabilities:
    tail_conf = exp( mean( lowest_10%_of(logprobs) ) )
    Why the bottom 10% instead of the overall average? Most tokens in a proof are predictable boilerplate ("Therefore,", " = ") with 99% probability. The actual reasoning mistakes happen at the handful of uncertain pivot steps where the model guesses a branch or drops a minus sign. Weighting votes by tail_conf automatically down-weights trajectories that hesitated.
  3. 3-of-4 Early Quorum Cancellation (DEER, ICLR 2026) + Conditional GenRM Verifier (arXiv:2504.01005): Waiting for the slowest of 4 parallel trajectories creates a long tail (one solver occasionally rambles for an extra 8 seconds). Using asyncio.wait(..., FIRST_COMPLETED), as soon as 3 solvers finish and agree unanimously with solid tail confidence, we immediately cancel the 4th straggler task, freeing the GPU slot and skipping verification altogether. Only when solvers genuinely disagree (3/16 = 19% of hard problems) do we run a targeted Generative Verifier (GenRM) pass that receives the competing derivations side-by-side, audits the exact algebra/edge cases step-by-step, and emits the verified answer.

Look at what happened on the exact problems where single-pass thinking failed:

  • Problem h04 (count integers ≤ 500 with 12 divisors, GT = 44): Greedy N = 1 answered 11 (×). Parallel solvers voted {'44': 3, '11': 1} (DeepConf share 73%), early-exited without even running the verifier, and returned 44 (✓).
  • Problem h11 (subtraction Nim game positions, GT = 57): Greedy N = 1 answered 54 (×). Parallel solvers split {'57': 2, '54': 1, '29': 1}, triggered the GenRM verifier, and returned 57 (✓).
  • Problem h03 (forbidden-point Catalan paths, GT = 42): Solvers split 4 ways (42, 132, 110, 106); both DeepConf tail confidence and the GenRM verifier singled out 42 (✓).

4. Two hyped ideas we tested, and reverted

Good engineering is as much about what you don't ship as what you do. Alongside the test-time compute engine, we implemented and tested two appealing C++ engine hypotheses directly from the papers database. Both failed cleanly under controlled benchmarks:

Negative Result #1: You cannot add extra loops to a fixed-horizon looped transformer

Our second production model, Inkling-2-Mini, is a looped transformer: its middle 12 layers execute 3 times in a loop per token (6 prelude + 12×3 looped core + 4 coda = 48 layer visits). Inspired by the 590-citation paper Scaling up Test-Time Compute with Latent Reasoning: A Recurrent Depth Approach (arXiv:2502.05171, Huginn), we asked: can we run Inkling-2-Mini with 4 loops for "free" latent thinking (zero extra VRAM), or 2 loops for extra speed?

Because Basalt's visit planner (walk.cpp) already supports arbitrary loop counts, adding a BASALT_INKLING_LOOPS override took 12 lines of C++ (disabling the DSpark drafter when loops != 3). We benchmarked loops = 2, 3, 4 on problems h01..h10:

CS takeaway: Models like Huginn generalize across loop counts because they are trained with randomized recurrence depth (sampling loop counts during pretraining) so the recurrent block learns to converge toward a fixed point. Inkling-2-Mini was trained at a fixed horizon of 3 passes, so pass 3 expects exact pass-2 activation statistics, stopping early or taking a 4th pass pushes hidden states off-manifold immediately. We reverted the override.

Negative Result #2: Why a "GPU-flat" speculative decoding prior hurt MoE prose

In speculative decoding, a tiny fast drafter guesses the next k tokens and the main model verifies all k tokens in one batched forward pass. Basalt's DraftPolicy uses an initial cost curve kShape assuming 16 tokens cost 6.4× as much as 1 token (which makes sense when experts spill to CPU RAM). Since both of our models fit 100% inside our RTX 5090's 32 GiB VRAM, we tested a flatter GPU-resident prior (inspired by Confidence-Modulated Speculative Decoding, arXiv:2508.15371) with lower probe thresholds.

On deterministic A/B runs (--seed 42, 2 repetitions):

  • On Inkling-2-Mini (128-expert Mixture-of-Experts, top-8 active per token), cold-start speed jumped (+71%) and warm structured JSON improved +5.1% (755 → 793 tok/s), but warm conversational decode dropped −3.9% and creative prose dropped −6.0% (321 → 302 tok/s).
  • Why? On a dense model, verifying 8 tokens reads the same weights as 1 token. On a sparse MoE model, 8 different tokens route to a much larger union of unique experts, so VRAM weight traffic genuinely scales with draft width! Over-drafting on unpredictable prose wasted bandwidth fetching experts for rejected tokens. Basalt's original online latency tracker already learns the exact hardware curve after warm-up, so we reverted our prior override.
